Technical write-up and user guide

Extending Distributional Analysis to Account for Employment and Growth Effects

The type of distributional analysis that guides debate around tax and benefit reform is always static. It measures how each household’s cash income changes on the day a policy changes, with behaviour held fixed. Given it can be done quickly and easily, it is this kind of analysis that think tanks publish and which dominates the discussion of the impact of a policy reform. However, many reforms are designed around a second round, for example from people moving into work, and a third, in which their work increases growth. Neither is counted for the households a reform affects. While academics do often test policy accounting for these long-run dynamic effects, they will only be published years after the event and restricted to larger questions.

Reforms that leave people worse off today but better off within a few years are therefore judged only by their losses. The static verdict on the 2025 cuts to PIP and the UC health element was negative, because the losses were large and the employment gains small, but a better-designed reform that replaced cash support with intensive help into work would be judged in the same restrictive way.

This briefing sets out a way to score the second and third rounds that can be done in a parsimonious way, using public data and official assumptions, and applies it to an illustrative reform for young people out of work losing access to £3,800 a year of benefits, and some end up in work as a result. We find that accounting for return-to-work effects changes the distributional picture for the people affected, and adding the growth effects changes the interpretation for whether society is better or worse off from the change.

The method is also available as an interactive online model, the Long-term model, which reproduces every result in this paper and lets users test their own assumptions. Section 4 is a guide to using it.

1.1 The first round

The first round is the morning-after figure produced by the distributional analysis of government and influential think tanks. It is the change in each household’s cash income on the day a reform takes effect, and it assumes nobody changes what they do.

1.2 The second round (work effect)

The second round adds the gain for people who move into work. We estimate how their pay and employment evolve from Understanding Society, as set out in Box 1, and calculate the gain for an archetype household, a single young adult renting their home, after income tax, National Insurance and the Universal Credit taper. For an individual this gives an income path once in work. For society we add these paths up across everyone affected, allowing for the share who move into work and the share who later leave.

Box 1: What we estimate from Understanding Society

Understanding Society interviews the same people every year.1 We link eleven waves, from 2009-10 to 2023-24, and find 6,571 occasions on which someone moved from not working into paid work. From these we estimate four things, by age and for people in poor health.

  • Starting pay and hours, around £8.90 an hour for 33 hours a week for 18 to 24-year-olds.
  • How fast pay grows after entry, measured for each person against average wage growth, over 4% a year faster than average for young entrants.
  • How many are still in work in each later year, around four in five for young entrants, counting those who leave and return.
  • The effect of poor health, around 10% lower pay and a higher chance of leaving work.

These estimates drive the second round directly, and the pay growth also sets how quickly new workers’ productivity catches up in the third.

1.3 The third round (growth effect)

The third round adds the extra output that new workers produce, following the OBR’s convention. If a new worker is as productive as the average worker, a 1% rise in employment raises GDP by 1%, which we use as a 1:1 benchmark. The OBR assumes people leaving inactivity produce around two-thirds as much as the average worker,2 so with average output of around £75,700 per worker,3 each new worker adds about £50,700 a year at first.

It would be wrong to assume they stay two-thirds as productive for the rest of their working life. Once in work their productivity should catch up with the national average, and we estimate that catch-up from their pay paths using Understanding Society data (explained above in Box 1), because pay and productivity move almost one for one. This is also why the OBR bases its ratios on relative pay, our approach extends this logic on through time. We estimate that a young entrant reaches the same pay growth levels as an average worker after around 14 years.

To turn output into living standards, we assume household incomes rise in line with GDP. A reform that lifts GDP by 0.1% raises everyone’s real income by 0.1%, on top of the gain new workers already receive from their own pay. Section 1.4 sets out how the choice of living standards measure affects the results.

1.4 Measures of living standards

The model offers three measures of living standards. Each is a reasonable way to value the extra output new workers produce, and they differ only in what they include.

  • GDP per head: Counts all the extra output new workers produce. It is the broadest measure.
  • Net national income: Counts the extra output less depreciation, the part that replaces worn-out equipment, and income paid abroad, about 87% of the total. It is the standard step from output to national income.
  • Household disposable income: Counts the share of extra output that reaches households after tax, about 60% of the total. It is closest to take-home income, but leaves out the Exchequer’s share, which funds public services or lower taxes. It is the default in the model.

The choice changes only the result for the group as a whole. In the illustrative reform the group is better off from year 2, 3 or 5 under each measure in turn, and £8.0 billion, £6.0 billion or £1.8 billion ahead after ten years. The individual and poverty results do not change, because they already assume incomes rise in proportion to GDP. The 87% and 60% shares are approximations from the national accounts.

2. Putting it together

We apply the three rounds to an illustrative reform. It replaces £3,800 a year of cash disability support for 200,000 young people aged 18 to 24 who are out of work with a health condition and spends the same amount on health and employment support. We assume in this hypothetical policy reform that a fifth move into work in year 2, above the 11-point employment gain found for the Future Jobs Fund,4 the strongest UK evidence for a job guarantee. Official statistics give the support no cash value, so everyone who stays out of work registers the full loss.

Across the group the first round removes £760 million a year from the aggregate living standards of the people affected, and the return to work recovers only a small part of it. This is because only around one in seven of the group is in work at any time, and the Universal Credit taper withdraws much of what each new worker earns, so their net gain of a few thousand pounds a year is small next to the £3,800 lost by everyone else.

Counting the extra output turns the total positive from year 2. By year 10 the reform adds about £1.9 billion a year to GDP, around 0.07%, and society is £8.0 billion ahead.

Our central case, two-thirds productivity rising with pay, is the most reasonable estimate for three reasons. Its starting point is the ratio the OBR states directly for people leaving inactivity. Its catch-up follows the OBR’s own logic and uses pay paths observed for real entrants. And its cap means new workers are never assumed to outperform people already in work.

The first round adds 39,000 young people to relative poverty in year one.5 The return to work removes about a quarter of that rise, and the result moves almost entirely with the share who find work. Around six in seven of the group are out of work at any time and keep the full loss, and each extra 10% moving into work removes roughly 3,900 people from poverty by year 10.

Growth does not move relative poverty, because it raises every income and the poverty line by the same proportion. It can only reduce poverty if its gains are redistributed, for example if the tax and benefit savings from each new worker, around £9,000 a year at first, fund more support for the group. Returned to the group, that would be worth £1,300 to £2,100 a head, offsetting a third to a half of the loss.

3. What this means for scoring reform

Static and dynamic scoring give very different answers about the same reform. On a static score this reform is a £3,800 a year loss for 200,000 young people and £760 million a year off household incomes. Once the return to work and the growth it brings are counted, people who find work are better off within three years and society comes out ahead from year 2.

Getting there does not need years of dynamic simulation. Each round uses public data and official assumptions, with Understanding Society showing what happens to people who move into work and the OBR’s productivity ratios giving the output they add. That makes it practical to publish alongside the static analysis for any reform designed to move people into work.

What matters depends on whose position is measured. For the individual, it is whether they find work and how their pay grows, while the growth dividend barely registers. For the affected group and for poverty, it is how many find work, whether the cash loss continues once they do, and whether any of the fiscal gain is returned to them, while growth makes no difference to relative poverty. For society, it is how many people move into work and how productive they are, while taxes and benefits only move money between households and the Exchequer.

The question a dynamic score answers is therefore not whether a reform has losers, but whether its employment effect is large enough, and its gains shared widely enough, to outweigh them. These estimates are illustrative rather than a full microsimulation, and the assumptions that matter most are set out in the appendix.

4. Using the online model

4.1 Model parameters

  • People affected, millions: The size of the group losing out, typed in directly.
  • Cash loss, £ a year: The first-round loss for each person, taken from static distributional analysis.
  • Share moving into work, %: The share of the group who move into work in year 2.
  • Group affected reflects: Average population assumes each new worker is as productive as the average worker, the 1:1 benchmark. Inactive population starts new workers at two-thirds of average output, the OBR ratio, and is the central case.
  • Productivity catch up: When on, new workers’ productivity rises with their pay towards the national average. When off, it stays at two-thirds, as the OBR assumes over its five-year horizon.
  • Average age when entering work: Applies the pay, job and catch-up paths estimated from Understanding Society for entrants aged 20, 30, 42 or 57. Effects stop at age 66.
  • Cash loss continues in work: Whether the first-round loss still applies once someone is in work, as it would for a benefit that is not means-tested.
  • Some entrants leave work: Whether to apply the share leaving work observed in Understanding Society. Turning it off shows a reform with strong in-work support.
  • Measure of living standards: How the extra output is valued in the group chart, as set out in section 1.4.

4.2 Outputs

Four headline figures sit at the top of the page, showing the year the entrant is better off, the year the group is better off, extra GDP a year by year 10, and the change in people in poverty in year 10.

Below them, three charts mirror sections 2.1 to 2.3. Each builds from the first round to the return to work and then growth, with each round shaded on top of the last. The individual and group charts can show either cumulative totals or each year’s effect, and the yearly view shows the productivity catch-up most clearly. Hovering over a chart shows the values for each year.

Appendix. Key assumptions

These are the assumptions in the method most open to challenge and most likely to change the results.

  • The first-round loss continues every year: It comes from static analysis and still applies after someone moves into work if the benefit lost is not means-tested.
  • One archetype household, a single adult renting: The gain from work uses 2024-25 tax and benefit rules for this household. Many young people live with their parents, so the first-round rise in poverty is likely to be overstated.
  • Pay growth is measured for people who stay in work: Understanding Society pay paths only track people still employed, so pay growth, and the productivity catch-up built on it, is likely to be overstated.
  • Some entrants leave work, as observed in the data: The share still in work each year comes from Understanding Society, including people who leave and return. If everyone stayed in work the growth effect would roughly double.
  • New workers start at two-thirds of average output and catch up: The starting ratio comes from the OBR and rises with measured pay, capped at average productivity. It is the largest single driver of the growth effect, and holding it fixed cuts that effect by around a quarter after ten years.
  • Capital keeps pace and nobody is displaced: Following the OBR, firms invest to equip new workers and existing workers keep their jobs. If capital did not respond, the short-run growth effect would be around 40% smaller.
  • Living standards rise in line with GDP: Every household’s income rises by the same proportion as GDP, which assumes a neutral fiscal stance and leaves relative poverty unchanged. Net national income and household disposable income count about 87% and 60% of the extra output instead.
  • Support in kind has no cash value: As in official statistics, services do not count as income, so swapping cash for support registers the full cash loss.
