How each question is handled

Group living standards

  • Starts from the year 1 cash change for each group, from the static analysis.
  • Each later year is compared with the static estimate held flat, so the chart shows what the employment and growth effects add.
  • The employment effect adds people moving into work, net of tax and Universal Credit withdrawal, without double counting anything in the year 1 estimate.
  • The growth effect takes the extra GDP from new workers, converts it to the chosen measure of living standards, removes their own wages, shares the rest equally across the UK population and gives the group its per-person share.

Poverty

  • Starts from the package's own year 1 poverty estimate.
  • Each later year is compared with the package estimate held flat. Under ‘Cohort keeps reform’ the effects are scaled to a group the size of the starting group.
  • The employment effect adds the remaining moves into work, phased in. The share of entrants who leave poverty is measured in Understanding Society households (74% on entering work, about 85% after two years), so in-work poverty is allowed for.
  • Growth is excluded from relative poverty, which it leaves unchanged.

Moves into work follow the timing observed in Understanding Society for 18 to 23-year-olds out of work: 33% of the five-year effect in year 1, 52% by year 2, 73% by year 3, 91% by year 4. Illustrative estimates rather than a full microsimulation.

Technical write-up

Extending distributional analysis to account for employment and growth effects

The analysis that shapes debate on welfare reform is static. It measures how a policy changes people’s cash incomes and the number of people in poverty on the day it takes effect, with behaviour held fixed. That is useful, transparent and quick to produce, which is why government and the leading think tanks rely on it. But it cannot show what happens next. If a reform moves people into work, their incomes rise, poverty falls and the economy grows, and none of that appears in the figures that frame the debate.

Researchers have long recognised the limits of a single snapshot. Work using UK panel data shows that judging reforms on longer-run incomes can change the picture substantially, because people move up and down the income distribution over time1. Applied to Universal Credit, a long-run measure finds the reform less regressive than a snapshot suggests2. What remains missing is a practical way to add the employment and growth responses to a reform, and to show them alongside the official figures.

The consequence is that a reform which costs some people today but leaves them better off within a few years is judged on the cost alone. Politicians narrow what they are willing to consider to changes that leave nobody worse off, and the case for growth is made separately, by the OBR, rarely joined up with the effect on people’s living standards.

This note sets out how we close that gap. We take the static analysis as given and add two effects over time, the gains for people who move into work and the extra output they produce. We apply both to two questions, whether the people affected are better or worse off each year and how the number of people in poverty changes. The evidence comes from a new analysis of Understanding Society and from the OBR’s own conventions, and each step is checked against the academic literature. The estimates are illustrative rather than a full microsimulation, and every assumption can be changed in the accompanying model.

1. Starting from the static analysis

We do not re-estimate the static analysis. We take its results as the starting point, drawn as an immediate change in year 0, so that every projection matches the official figures at the outset and any difference later on comes only from the effects we add. This is a deliberate choice. It keeps the method compatible with any distributional model, whether the government’s own or one run by a think tank, and makes clear which parts of the result are ours.

The static analysis can be entered as a single average cash change for the people affected, or as a full breakdown by income decile in the style of published analysis, which the model collapses into the same average. Up to three poverty estimates can be entered side by side, for example those of DWP, the IFS and the Resolution Foundation, so that our employment effect can be compared against each.

Two choices about the policy itself matter from the start. The first is how the reform is held over time. A change that applies only while people are young fades as they age out of it, while one that applies to all new entrants grows until it covers everyone on the system. The second is whether the static analysis already includes some movement into work. Departmental analysis sometimes does, so the model lets the user say whether none, the first year or the full employment response is already counted, and adds only what is missing.

2. Building the evidence from Understanding Society

Projecting what happens after someone moves into work needs evidence that follows the same people over many years. Understanding Society, the UK’s largest household panel survey, does exactly that, interviewing the members of around 40,000 households every year3. Turning it into estimates that a model can use is a substantial undertaking, of the kind normally carried out by a dedicated research team over several months.

We linked eleven waves of individual and household records from 2009-10 to 2023-24, around 466,000 person-year observations. From these we identified every move from not working into paid work. For each, we derived hourly pay from monthly earnings and usual hours, put all values into 2024 prices, and followed the person through later waves to measure how their pay grows relative to pay across the economy, separately for those entering work at different ages and for those in poor health. We tracked 13,034 spells out of work among young people, allowing for people leaving the survey, to measure how long moves into work take.

We then linked each person to their household’s net income in every wave, adjusted it for household size and compared it with 60% of that year’s median, before and after housing costs. This let us observe whether moving into work actually took a household out of poverty, rather than assume it. Most young people who move into work live with others, often their parents, so household income gives a far more realistic picture than a single person living alone. Charts 1 and 2 show the pay paths the model uses. People who move into work young see their pay rise much faster than those who enter later. A 20-year-old’s real pay is around 50% higher after ten years in work, against around 10% for someone entering at 42. Because the gain from work grows with pay, the youngest entrants gain most from staying in work, while older entrants start from a similar gain that rises only slowly.

These pay paths carry one known bias. Pay growth can only be measured for people who stay in work, and those who stay are likely to be the ones whose pay is rising, so the paths probably overstate progression for the average entrant. Against that, the model does not count the lasting damage that long spells out of work do to young people’s pay. Following men who were unemployed when young, Gregg and Tominey find a pay penalty of 13 to 21% at age 42, falling to around 9 to 11% for those who avoid further unemployment4. Getting young people into work earlier avoids part of that scar, a long-run gain the model leaves out.

3. The employment effect

The employment effect follows the same sequence for any package.

  • Who moves into work. A share of the people affected moves into work, taken from the package’s own employment estimate. We subtract any part the static analysis already counts, so nothing is counted twice.
  • When they move. Moves build up over about five years, following the timing in Table 2, rather than arriving all at once.
  • What they gain. Each new worker follows the Understanding Society pay path for their age, after income tax, National Insurance and the Universal Credit taper. On entry, about three-fifths of extra gross pay is lost to tax and benefit withdrawal, so a young entrant earning around £15,400 a year is about £6,000 better off.
  • Whether they stay. Entrants are assumed to stay in work, reflecting the in-work support that accompanies most packages. The model can instead apply the exits observed in Understanding Society.

The timing matches the international evidence. A review of more than 200 evaluations of employment programmes finds effects close to zero at first, growing over the following two to three years, and larger for people entering from long spells without work5. UK evidence points the same way. The Future Jobs Fund, which offered subsidised jobs to young people out of work, left participants about 11 percentage points more likely to be in unsubsidised work two years after starting6.

The size of the response also has a basis in the evidence on disability benefits. When the Netherlands tightened disability insurance, people replaced around 62 cents of every euro lost through higher earnings, a response that held steady over time, and a further 30 cents at first through other benefits7. In the United States, applicants on the margin of being awarded disability benefits would have been 28 percentage points more likely to work without them, with no effect for those with the most severe conditions and up to 50 percentage points for those with less severe ones8. That is why the model applies the employment response only to people whose conditions allow work, not to those with the most severe conditions.

With the illustrative package, the employment effect recovers about four-fifths of the group’s cash loss within ten years, close to the Dutch earnings rebound. Our model does not allow for people moving onto other benefits, so the immediate loss may be overstated. The OBR’s own analysis is a reminder not to assume too much. It estimates that someone in ill health is 28% less likely to be in the labour force than someone in good health, so even large improvements in health or support translate only partly into work9. The share moving into work is therefore the input that most needs to come from the package itself.

4. The growth effect

New workers produce extra output. Following the OBR, we assume firms invest to equip them, so output rises with employment rather than being spread more thinly across the same capital, and we extend that assumption beyond the OBR’s five-year horizon. This is a key OBR assumption and it does much of the work in any estimate of growth from extra employment. In its scoring of labour supply measures, the OBR assumes the capital stock adjusts over its forecast period so that capital per worker is unchanged, which means output rises in proportion to the extra labour. If capital did not respond, each extra worker would add less, and with labour taking around 60% of output the short-run growth effect would be around 40% smaller. The extra output is converted to household disposable income, the new workers’ own pay is taken out because it is already counted in the employment effect, and the rest is shared across the population, with the group receiving its share. Growth counts towards living standards and absolute poverty. It does not change relative poverty, because it raises incomes and the poverty line together.

The challenge is applying the OBR’s principles to a group that is only part of the population. The OBR assumes people returning from inactivity are around two-thirds as productive as the average worker, a ratio that reflects the pay of a typical adult leaving work through ill health10. Our group is young and low-paid, earning around 40% of average pay on entry. Applied directly, the OBR ratio would have each young entrant producing about three times their pay, and would imply more extra GDP per job than the OBR’s own rule of thumb that 0.3% more employment adds about 0.2% to GDP11.

We therefore start new workers at half of average output per worker and let their output rise with their measured pay towards the average. This reproduces the OBR’s overall relationship between jobs and output, 0.65% more GDP for each 1% more employment against the OBR’s 0.67%, so the growth effect is consistent with how the OBR would score the same change in employment.

The academic evidence supports the shape of this profile and suggests the level is on the generous side. Studies that link firms’ output to the ages of their workers find productivity rising with age into the forties and early fifties, with wages rising in line with productivity at younger ages12. Similar work in Dutch manufacturing finds little gap between pay and productivity at any age13, and Canadian data suggest young men may produce somewhat more than they are paid14. Taken together, this points to young workers’ output being nearer 1.7 to 2 times their pay than the 2.5 times our central case implies. Linking output directly to pay gives a cautious lower bound, which the model offers as an alternative.

Our central case assumes new workers do not displace others, in line with the OBR’s view that over time extra labour supply raises employment roughly one for one as wages, investment and hiring adjust. In the short run that may not hold. A large randomised trial of job search help for young people in France found the gains were transitory and came partly at the expense of other jobseekers, especially in weak labour markets15. At the scale of the illustrative package, a short-run sensitivity of around 25% displacement, fading over five years, is a sensible test.

5. Poverty

The poverty projection starts from the package’s own immediate estimate, so it always matches the official figure at the start. The employment effect then removes people from poverty as they move into work. In each year, the fall in poverty is the extra new workers, times the share of them in poverty while out of work, times the share who leave it on entering work.

We use the rates measured in Understanding Society households rather than assuming every job ends poverty. This reflects a well-established finding in UK research. Movement into work, followed by rising pay, is the most common route out of poverty, but low-paid and insecure work does not guarantee it16. Long-run analysis of the British panel data shows how much movement in and out of poverty there is from year to year, and how often exits are short-lived17. Among households already in work, around seven in ten exits from in-work poverty come with a rise in earnings18, which is why pay progression after entry matters for poverty as well as for incomes.

The model can show poverty on either basis, using the matching rates, and can show relative or absolute poverty. Growth affects only the absolute measure, by raising incomes against a line fixed in real terms.

6. What the model shows

With the illustrative package, the people affected start £3.1 billion a year worse off. As moves into work build up, the employment effect closes most of that gap, and the group is better off each year from around year 15. Poverty falls from the static estimate of 20,000 to around 103,000 fewer people in poverty by year 10. The economy is around £57 billion a year larger by year 10, close to 2% of GDP, because extra workers add output. Most of that gain is shared across the whole population rather than kept by the group, so the group’s living standards depend mainly on moving into work, unless government chooses to direct the fiscal gains to the people the reform affects.

These results rest on observed patterns rather than the measured effect of a specific policy. The size of the employment response, the number of new entrants and the productivity of new workers matter most. Pay growth after entry is likely to be overstated for the average entrant, while the long-run benefit of avoiding scarring is left out. Displacement is assumed away in the central case. The value of the approach is that it makes each of these assumptions visible and testable, checks them against the evidence, and joins up the effect of a reform on people’s incomes with its effect on the economy, which static analysis on its own cannot do.
